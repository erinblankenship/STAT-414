4  Stratified Sampling

In the last chapter, we noted that an SRS isn’t the best option if we have additional information that could help us come up with a more cost-effective sampling design. One such option is stratified sampling.

4.1 Overview of Stratified Sampling

TipBasic Concept: Stratification

The idea of stratification is








For example,






The basic set-up for stratified sampling is as follows:



What should we be thinking about to decide whether or not to stratify?

  • Is there a chance we could draw a really bad sample?





  • Do we want to draw conclusions about a particular subgroup of the population (especially with a particular precision)?







  • Is it more convenient or cheaper?






  • Can we construct the strata in a way that our estimates will be more precise?






If the answer to any of the above is yes, stratification is probably a good idea.

With simple random samples, we only had one design feature we needed to determine: the sample size \(n\). With stratified sampling, we need to determine:




It’s actually easier to look at these in reverse order. We’ll start by assuming the strata have already been defined, and consider how to allocate observations among them.

4.5.1 Allocation in Stratified Random Sampling

The general set-up is













There are several options for determining how many observations to sample in each stratum. Some allocation strategies deal with the whole population, others deal with subpopulations. Yet others are a compromise between the two. The best allocation strategy depends on the goals of the study.

Example: Say you have 5 strata and can afford a total of \(n=4000\) observations.
